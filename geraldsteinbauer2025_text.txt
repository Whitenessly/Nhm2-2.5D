Abstract
With the latest technological advances, robotics is now also developing ever more
rapidly. Demand for the automation of dangerous, monotonous and physically dif-
ficult work is increasing day by day. As a result, there is more and more interest
in robots that can drive automatically in complex terrain. This thesis focuses on
planning methods that enable a robot to drive autonomously in off-road situations.
Most of the existing literature focuses on path planning in 2D, where the 3D world
is converted to a 2D grid map. The value of a cell determines the cost of traversal
at that location. There is little literature on methods that can natively utilize 3D
or 2.5D information and thus provide a less compressed representation of the envi-
ronment for planning. In this work, methods from both groups were systematically
compared. Different simulators and physics drivers were examined in order to enable
a realistic evaluation in a simulation. Perlin-noise was used to generate the realistic
terrain. For the methods using a 2D view, classical and widely used planning algo-
rithms were used. To generate the map for the classical methods, a 2.5D map was
converted into a 2D map by converting the slopes of the 2.5D map into costs. The
search algorithms then find a path based on costs. To test a method that uses native
2.5D data for planning, a new approach was developed that uses the robot’s foot-
print to cost its calculated orientations on the 2.5D elevation map. This method uses
the RRT∗ algorithm, which considers different locations on the 2.5D map by random
sampling and considers the orientation of the footprint for each position to generate
the cost. The tests showed that the proposed method, which uses 2.5D data directly,
planned shorter and faster paths in most scenarios, while the journey remained safe
and reliable for the robot. The results for the classical methods showed that they
are especially useful in scenarios where little computational power is available, and
even if they plan longer paths, they are still useful in less complex scenarios. At the
end of the work, further extension possibilities were discussed, which will be added
in the future to take into account, among other things, different surface features on
the terrain. 1

Chapter 1
Introduction
Figure 1.1: The Artus climbing a small hill on unstructured terrain.
1.1 Motivation
21st century, at the forefront of robotics, one of the challenges robotics research is
facing is making robots that can navigate the world with human-like proficiency.
Humans take a profound semantic understanding and perception of the world for
granted. It is easy for the human brain to decide the next step without falling and
reason about possible moves that lead to a valid route without injury or other con-
sequences. Although self-driving cars exist and already work well, they have the
advantage of having a semi-controlled environment with paved roads, traffic lights,
traffic rules, signs, and other aids. It is also much easier for humans to navigate in
urban environments because some of the load is taken off the brain by having signs
that tell us what to do and roads to follow. Hiking a mountain, going into a cave,
or just generally visiting a non-urban environment where we’ve never been before is
1
--- PAGE 11 ---
a much harder task. We become extra careful and watch our step between rocks,
through a river, or in the forest, and derive a physically feasible route. This is a task
that doesn’t have a general solution in the field of robotics at the time of writing. To
get closer to a solution to this problem, this thesis will present and compare methods
for planning paths in off-road environments, particularly on uneven surfaces, to better
understand the state-of-the-art. The main motivation for this work is that there’s no
openly available generalized solution to autonomously let a robot drive in a forest,
hiking path, or off-road terrain using the same ”paths“ as humans do. The problem
that’s investigated in this thesis will be simplified to getting from a start to a goal
position on uneven terrains with the perfect perception of the environment. This
problem has different components, including sensing, planning, and acting. Due to
the fact that the investigation of a complete perception, planning, and control stack
would be out of the scope of this thesis, perfect perception will be assumed, and the
work will focus on planning a path in a static environment. The presented solutions
will be designed in a way such that they can work in real time, on real hardware, and
in realistic scenarios.
This project targets a use case where a robot should be able to transport ammunition,
food, or even injured soldiers for the Austrian military in off-road environments, such
as forests, hiking paths, and military practice fields. The ARTUS from CharismaTec
[1] shown in Figure 1.1 is an off-road robot used in this project that is capable of ad-
justing its kinematic features to adapt itself to the terrain. In the future, this robot
should be able to drive autonomously on the same routes and terrain the soldiers use.
1.2 Problem definition
This thesis aims to investigate and evaluate different path-planning methods suitable
for navigating with a 4-wheeled non-holonomic robot on rough and challenging ter-
rain. This could be done in many different ways, but the outcome can be constrained
by some assumptions and requirements. Before going through these requirements, a
few established concepts of robotics have to be introduced.
Path planning is usually divided into hierarchical stages, which are global planning,
local planning, and control. Global planning focuses on the big picture, usually by
planning on a static map known a priori. Local planning focuses on a small area
around the robot during mission execution to increase performance and reaction
times, and its main task is to avoid dynamic obstacles that aren’t present on the
static map. The controller is responsible for accurately tracking the paths produced
by the global and local planners. In the last few years, the clear lines between these
hierarchical stages have become more blurry, some research fuses the global and local
planning stages, while others keep the global planner, but use an all-in-one solution
for local planning and control. The methods used in this thesis also mix global and
local planning. The tests conducted will use the algorithms in a global fashion on a
2
--- PAGE 12 ---
static map, however their performance should allow for quick re-planning in future
work to avoid dynamic obstacles.
In robotics, traversability defines an area of terrain or road that a ground robot can
safely drive or walk on (traverse), respecting its limits. Off-road ground robotics’
primary focus is finding traversable areas in complex situations and terrains.
The problem is that classical planning pipelines usually constrain the information
to a binary map, where obstacles are labeled as lethal or non-lethal, indicating the
traversability at that area by the risk of collision. This method works well for simple,
mostly even environments, like indoor corridors or flat streets, but makes navigating
on uneven terrain very difficult because it compresses an uneven surface into a binary
representation, where an area is either occupied and untraversable or free. This leads
to missed opportunities, where with more data, a safer, shorter, and quicker path
could be produced, and in some cases, it might even prevent planning altogether if no
non-lethal area remains. Using such a representation also makes it harder to take the
physical properties of the robot into account while planning on off-road areas. There
isn’t any best-practice, generalized way of representing and planning on uneven ter-
rain in robotics at the time of writing, however lot’s of techniques exist which allow
for better environmental awareness.
1.3 Contribution
The goal of this thesis is to compare planning methods in kinematically challeng-
ing, accurately simulated off-road environments to gain a better understanding of the
properties and limits of path planning algorithms for 2.5D environmental representa-
tions. As 2.5D perception goes beyond the scope of this thesis it will be assumed to be
perfect throughout the tests. To simulate motion in a virtual environment, accurate
physics and simulation techniques are needed. Although the Artus shown in Figure
1.1 was not available as a kinematically accurate model at the time of conducting
this project, a widely used substitute has been found, namely the Clearpath Husky
[2]. The Husky is a rigid-body wheeled differential-drive robot specifically designed
for off-road terrain. There are also a lot of resources available to be able to set up a
realistic simulation for the Husky. Nonetheless, the tests that are conducted will be
designed in a way that they can be re-run as soon as a model of the Artus is available.
With this in mind, the contribution of this work can be summarized as follows:
• An overview of currently available tools and techniques that allows a robot to
take more information about the surrounding environment into account and
plan a feasible path for challenging terrain.
• Implementation of a method which compresses 2.5D data to a 2D costmap with
minimized loss of information, that can be used by standard search algorithms
• A method using 2.5D information natively to calculate traversability

• A simulation pipeline with realistic physics and terrain generation to ensure
close-to-life results
• An evaluation method that is flexible and can be easily adapted to other path
planning techniques in the future for further research
The main objective of this work is to gain a deeper understanding of current path-
planning methods for robots operating on rough and challenging terrains. This in-
volves a comprehensive review of relevant literature to contextualize the problem
within existing research, followed by the implementation and testing of selected mo-
tion planning approaches in a simulated environment.
1.4 Structure
The work is divided into different sections:
• In Chapter 2 Sections 2.1.1 to 2.2.4, an overview is given to describe the basic
tools and algorithms used for planning on uneven terrain. Research is con-
ducted on the currently available planning pipelines to gather ideas on how
basic planning principles can be extended to be aware of uneven terrain.
• In Sections 2.3 to 2.3.2 research is conducted to choose a method to simulate
realistic physics in off-road environments using wheeled robots.
• In the next Sections, 2.4 to 2.4.2 research is conducted to find a terrain genera-

Chapter 2
Overview and research of path
planning on uneven terrain
For planning a path, in general the robotics community has a broad range of well-
established methods. Moving robots more or less autonomously has been a major
research field in robotics since the middle of the 1960s. Starting with Shakey [3],
the mobile robot that could reason about its environment and move autonomously,
the field has been in a non-stop search for better, more efficient, and more intelligent
algorithms. Shakey was basically the first robot, or more appropriately called, ”Au-
tomata“ of its category, originally developed for a proposal funded by the Defense
Advanced Research Projects (DARPA) [4], with the goal to create an autonomous
reconnaissance device. One of the many results of this research is still widely used
in the field of computer science after almost 60 years, namely the A∗ [5] algorithm,
which was developed specifically for this project to enable Shakey to find paths effi-
ciently.
The field has fortunately come a long way, and lots of research has been done in
the past few decades. As technology advanced and more powerful computers have
been developed the possibilities of planning have also been greatly expanded. While
efficiency is still a big concern when designing a path planner, it is less of an issue
nowadays due to the high computational power of even the smallest edge computers
used in simpler robots. Advances in formulating and solving search and optimization
problems have finally enabled serious research in path planning in higher dimensions
for drones and off-road vehicles, which would have been impossible previously.
In Sections 2.1.1 and 2.1.7, two papers about path planning and mapping in off-
road scenarios will be presented, which are used to form the basis of this thesis.
The first paper, titled ”Survey of Trajectory Planning Algorithms for Off-Road Un-
crewed Ground Vehicles“ [6] presented at the Modeling and Simulation for Au-
tonomous Systems Conference 2023 by Gargano et al., gives a great overview of
commonly used algorithms in path planning, their advantages and drawbacks, even

in combination with higher dimensionality and different types of controllers. The
authors provide an overview of commonly used algorithms, separating them into dif-
ferent groups and explaining their advantages and disadvantages.
Based on the author’s recommendations, some algorithms are selected from this paper
to be used in this thesis and described in more detail to understand their function.
This includes the algorithms A∗, Theta∗, D∗ RRT, and RRT∗, which allows the inves-
tigation of both basic graph-search and sampling-based algorithms and their improved
iterations.
The second paper is titled ”Unmanned Ground Vehicle (UGV) Path Planning in 2.5D
and 3D“ [7]. This publication from the Army Engineer Research and Development
Center in Alexandria(Virginia, USA) by Ennasr et al. presents the state-of-the-art
methods of using 2.5D and 3D information for path planning. The paper shows three
methods for mapping and navigation: one on traditional 2D maps, one using 2.5D
elevation mapping [8], and the last one using a fully 3D approach called mesh nav-

that can be used in real-time while taking uneven terrain into account efficiently. The
first technique uses a 2D compressed representation of the environment, called a 2D

detail to show their advantages and disadvantages. The third technique, called mesh
navigation, which uses a full 3D map of the environment, will not be investigated
further, as it can only use information available a priori and isn’t usable in real-time
at the time of writing this thesis.

2.1 Overview of off-road planning methods
2.1.1 Survey of Trajectory Planning Algorithms for Off-Road Un-
crewed Ground Vehicles [6]
The authors, Gargano et al., wrote this paper to guide the readers in choosing an
algorithm for an off-road path planning problem. Their argumentation and the se-
lection system they presented will now be described in more detail.
It is important to mention that this paper also talks about reinforcement and
deep learning approaches, which this thesis will not cover. Machine learning-based
path planning approaches are still in a very early stage with no clear ”best practice“
and it could be its own thesis to train, test, and compare them, which makes them
out of scope for this project. As the paper also mentions, Machine learning-based
approaches are less generalizable, often requiring re-training if the kinematics change
or if the environment is special. Their behavior is also hard to predict in unknown
areas and situations in which they weren’t trained. This makes them less robust in
real scenarios currently.
The paper separates planning methods into different algorithm types. First, the
authors address graph-based search algorithms, more specifically A ∗. A ∗ will be ex-
plained in more detail in the next section. The authors address the main problem
with A∗, which is,that it can only plan on grid intersections, restricting the direction
to multiples of 45 degrees, and a complete re-iteration of the map if a new plan is
requested. This poses a problem especially in the case, where the prepared grid map
forces A∗ to make kinematically feasible turns, which requires a large number of nodes
to iterate through. For this reason, many other variants of A ∗ have been developed,
of which Theta ∗ and D ∗ will be explained in detail in the next sections. These al-
gorithms introduce additional features to mitigate some of the known constraints of
A∗.
The next group of algorithms is the sampling-based algorithms. These algorithms
sample the configuration space randomly and only check for collisions on the transi-
tion between sampled points or calculate costs based on other features at the given
point. One of the most popular sampling algorithms is RRT (Rapidly-exploring Ran-
dom Tree). With RRT, the random sample points are connected together in a tree
9
--- PAGE 19 ---
structure. A connection of nodes can occur in many different feasible scenarios, but
the most popular method is just distance and whether an obstacle is in the way or
not. Another sampling-based algorithm mentioned in the paper is PRM (probabilis-
tic roadmaps), which, due to its similarities to RRT, will not be discussed in detail.
PRM is generally considered to perform worse than RRT due to its 2-stage planning
metho, however it still provides good results in higher dimensional space, and some-
times provides better results than RRT, especially when used with industrial robotic
arms. Both algorithms only provide suboptimal paths. For both algorithms, it is true
that the more sampled points are allowed, the better the resulting path will be. This
also means that with fewer points, a less kinematically feasible path will be generated,
with sharp turns and straights with perturbations and deviations. This problem can
be addressed either by some smoothing algorithm like B´ ezier curves [15] or by using
and tuning a local planner or controller to ignore abrupt deviations. There are also
several extensions and variants of both of these algorithms addressing this weakness.
One of the most notable and widely used variants of RRT is RRT ∗, which improves
upon its drawbacks. This algorithm will be explained along with RRT in detail in
the next sections.
One of the most field-proven algorithm of this category is GBPlanner v2.0 used in
the 2021 DARPA challenge. This planner uses a bifurcated local and global planner
architecture but is essentially a sampling-based algorithm at its basis. This planner
is, however, mainly used for a priori exploration rather than executing actual tra-
jectories. This planner was used along with ArtPlanner [10] of ANYbotics [11] to
win the DARPA Subterranean Challenge in 2021. In the next section, both of these
algorithms will be explained further.
The paper also deals with reactive and optimization-based planning algorithms. These
algorithms solve an optimization problem at each time step, focusing on traversal time
and path length, while taking collisions and dynamic obstacles into account. Due to
their high computational overhead, both reactive and optimization-based algorithms
are mostly used to generate short paths as local planners or controllers, although
they could be used globally by providing them with the direction of the goal. This,
however, severely restricts the robot’s ability to find an optimal path, and thus, most
of the time, they are combined with some other algorithm like RRT ∗ or A ∗ to solve
the global planning problem first.
The authors describe two examples of reactive algorithms, which are artificial po-
tential field (APF) [16] and dynamic window approach (DW A) [17]. APF is a virtual
forces approach that generates a virtual force to attract a robot towards a goal and
repel it away from obstacles. This approach assigns a value to each point in the
environment such that the robot moves along the least repulsive way. APF is easy to
compute, however it tends to generate local minima and struggles in narrow passages.
DW A(Dynamic Window Approach), as the name suggests, uses a dynamic window of
10
--- PAGE 20 ---
feasible velocities to guide the robot towards the goal. The prediction horizon, that
this windows is calculated on is configurable, however the farther it is the less accu-
rate it becomes. This algorithm also might get stuck in local minima, like U-shaped
obstacles.
Two examples of optimization-based algorithms from the paper are model predic-
tive control (MPC) [18] and reachability-based trajectory design (RTD) [19]. MPC
iteratively generates a path while executing a previous plan, solving an optimization
problem at each step. It basically predicts the future configuration of the system
for the next few time steps and solves an optimization problem to select the best
sequence of actions on the planning horizon.
RTD also requires solving an optimization problem, but this time, it’s a polynomial
problem at each iteration. This means that the difficulty increases the more con-
straints and the more number of optima are introduced. Although it is not the most
popular optimization-based algorithm, a version of it is used in the award-winning
ArtPlanner [10], which will be described in more detail later.

2.1.3 A ∗
As mentioned in the introduction, A∗ is still the most used algorithm even after half
a century. This, of course, has good reasons, namely the fact that it works really
well in most 2D scenarios. A∗ was originally developed by Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson,
and Bertram Raphael of Stanford Research Institute and is basically an extension to
Dijkstra’s algorithm [20]. Dijkstra works by iterating through every cell of a map (or
every edge of a graph), expanding evenly in every direction from the start point, and
finding a path to the goal with the lowest cost. The cost, in most cases, is the length
of the path. Both of these algorithms guarantee an optimal path - if it exists - at
the end. The difference with A∗, however, is that it takes the Euclidian distance to
the goal as a heuristic cost, making the expansion process always ”grow“towards the
goal, while Dijkstra expands in every direction equally. This makes A∗ considerably
faster and more efficient, which is why it’s still widely used and new expansions are
developed. At each iteration A ∗ chooses the path which minimizes:
f (n) = g(n) + h(n) (2.1)
Where g(n) is the cost to get from the start node to node n and h(n) is the heuristic
cost that estimates the least costly path from n to the goal node. The heuristic cost
can be modified to the needs of the problem, however, in the mint A ∗ algorithm, this
cost is always a function of the Euclidian distance between node n and the goal:
D(start, goal) =

(goalx − startx)2 + (goaly − starty)2 (2.2)
in 2 dimensions. As can be seen from the cost function, the A ∗ algorithm could be
modified to plan in 3 dimensions, however this increases the complexity significantly.
This approach would work as a simple solution for unmanned aerial vehicles (UA Vs),
which would guarantee the shortest collision-free 3D path, but not a kinematically
feasible one.
In the case of ground robotics, there’s either an additional check whether the next
node is a lethal obstacle and, if so, then skipping that node entirely, or the cost is
increased in the cost function to the maximum. There’s also an ”in between“, where,
e.g., a non-binary gradient cost on a cost map can be taken into account, which often
leads to a smoother, more elegant path at some additional computational overhead
during map preparation. This topic will be described in greater detail in Section
2.1.8. An example of A∗ avoiding an obstacle can be seen in Figure 2.3.

A∗ can only plan cell-by-cell, which means that kinematically infeasible paths are

this reason, this algorithm is never used in isolation but rather together with either

2.1.4 D ∗
A∗ has been the basis of many algorithms in the past few years, improving upon its
technique and modifying the approach to make it more efficient.
D∗ [21] from Anthony Stentz is one of such improvements, which is a widely used
algorithm in the field of robotics nowadays and has lots of variations with different
features.
D∗ starts planning from the goal position instead of the start, propagating in a wave-
like manner towards the goal. This has the advantage that when the whole area is
covered, D∗ basically has an A ∗ optimal solution for every possible start point on the
map. This means that if an obstacle comes in the way, the path can be switched
almost instantly to another optimal one. While re-planning specifically won’t be
tested in this work, the time it takes D ∗ to plan in complex environments is still an
interesting metric for future work. Figure 2.4 shows the wave-like propagation from
the goal position on top to the start on the bottom. In comparison to A∗, D∗ keeps all
costs in a list, which allows it to quickly choose a new path if a cost changes, e.g. due
to a new obstacle. This property is where it has gotten its name from, Dynamic-A ∗.

2.1.5 Theta ∗
Theta∗ is almost indistinguishable from A∗ on the first look. The main difference is,
however, that Theta∗ is an any-angle algorithm, meaning that the planning direction
of the next step is not constrained to a neighboring cell but can be any other cell
as long as there is line-of-sight between the two nodes. Line-of-sight is given, if no
obstacle can be found between two arbitrary nodes. This means that it can find much
shorter paths in some situations, where A∗ would otherwise have to go node-by-node
on the grid. The difference is illustrated well in the original Theta∗ paper from Daniel
et al. [22].

2.1.6 RRT and RRT ∗
Next, a sampling-based method will be described, along with its improved variant,
RRT, and RRT ∗. All of the previously described algorithms had the same basic
principle: they build up a graph node-by-node on some map representation. This
means they have to go step-by-step to reach the goal, which means that the further
away the goal is, the longer it takes for them to compute a path. To solve this
problem, LaValle et al. developed Rapidly Exploring Random Trees [23], which
has many extensions nowadays and is a widely used algorithm in robotics. A basic
example of RRT is shown in Figure 2.7.
16
--- PAGE 26 ---
Figure 2.7: The expansion process of an example RRT configuration shown in the the paper
by LaValle et al. [23].
RRT randomly samples the state space- or configuration space for a new state. In
the case of map grids, this is just a random coordinate pair. However, this statement
already implies that RRTs have a lot of potential for other use cases as well. After a
sample is drawn, a connection to the nearest neighbor is attempted. What makes this
approach powerful is that the criteria for this connection to happen are customizable.
In the standard implementation, and in most use cases, this criterion is just having
free space between the two nodes. This is enough for most indoor robotics applica-
tions and object avoidance.
The space is usually sampled uniformly, without revisiting already sampled nodes,
which means, that the probability of expanding the tree is higher in unvisited, free
areas. This makes the tree naturally grow towards unvisited space, making the
approach to the goal position faster. RRT was partially inspired by probabilis-
tic roadmaps(PRM). PRMs, however, require multiple connections between sampled
states, while RRT doesn’t require any, making it faster in most situations.
The only problem with the standard RRT implementation is that it is only probabilistic-
complete, meaning it will always find a path, if there’s any and enough time is given,
but not necessarily an optimal one.
To solve the problem of optimality, RRT∗ was introduced by S. Karaman and E. Fraz-
zoli in [24]. Their modification introduces a novel rewiring method, which doesn’t
necessarily connect new samples to the nearest neighbor but rather to the neighbor,
which leads to the least costly path along the way. The cost can still be any con-
straint, not just distance. Given enough time and enough number of samples, this
algorithm converges towards an optimal solution, which makes it asymptotically op-
timal. The authors detail this process as well as other extensions they’ve made to
other algorithms in [24]. With RRT and RRT ∗, the dimensionality of the problem
matters less than in the case of graph-search algorithms. The random samples can
be of any dimension, and edges can have any constraints. This also does not increase
the complexity of the algorithm, as this is only linearly dependent on the number of
samples (O(n)). This property makes these algorithms very efficient and fast, espe-
cially for higher dimensional spaces and problems. The following two figures show
a demonstration of the two algorithms in a simulator made by A. T. Becker and L.
Huang [25].

2.1.7 Unmanned Ground Vehicle (UGV) Path Planning in 2.5D and
3D [7]
Before jumping into certain algorithms and systems in detail, another key paper for
this thesis is briefly presented: ”Unmanned Ground Vehicle (UGV) Path Planning in
2.5D and 3D“ [7] by Ennasr et al.
As the paper states, full autonomy requires some kind of map or environmental
representation for the robot. This is either provided before the mission or in real
time using SLAM. In the case of 2D costmaps, the sensor data is basically mapped
onto costs in cells on the map, and neutral and lethal costs are defined. In the case
of the Robot Operating System(ROS) framework [26] this is a value from 0 to 255, 0
representing completely unoccupied and 255 representing lethal cost. Inflation layers
can then add some padding to these costmaps to account for sensor noise and other
disturbances and to keep the robot far enough from a lethal obstacle, accounting for
its footprint. A rough road with e.g. small, sharp rocks would be unnoticeable on
such a map due to details being lost, as they are either classified as free if too fine
or occupied if their height reaches the threshold. 2.5D maps, on the other hand,
incorporate height data into the map, such that a road with high variations in the
topology can be associated with a higher cost, making the planner avoid it. Both of
these approaches, however, suffer from the fact that in the case of a high-dimensional
floating object, like a bridge, it is difficult to determine if a path is underneath or
above said bridge. Complete 3D maps solve this issue but at the cost of very high
computational demands. The authors of the paper Unmanned Ground Vehicle (UGV)
Path Planning in 2.5D and 3D [7] only explore 2.5D and 3D methods, as 2D repre-
sentations don’t offer enough topological information. Using a 2D map, an otherwise
18
--- PAGE 28 ---
non-lethal obstacle (like a small hill) might be marked as lethal due to its height,
while it would still be easily traversable by the robot and could be chosen as a viable
route if no better option is available. To mitigate this ”loss of data“ the authors
describe 2 approaches, one of which uses grid maps along with the the elevation map-
ping [27] library from the ETH Z ¨urich, and the other one using mesh navigation [9].
The approach to use grid maps will be presented later in this thesis, and only a brief
description of the author’s methods will be given now.
The grid map library from Frankhauser et al. provides different functions to con-
vert the produced map into different representations. The authors use the occupancy
grid conversion function to create a 2D costmap from the 2.5D grid map with eleva-
tion data. They test this approach on the Clearpath inspection demo world using the
Clearpath Husky as shown in Figure 2.9.
Figure 2.9: Raw grid map with elevation values on the left side, and a converted 2D costmap
on the right shown by Enassr et al. in [7].
While this approach works for a completely static map with known elevation val-
ues, it cannot be used for terrain with changing relative heights, because the range of
the cost is given by a minimum and maximum elevation value, which stays absolute.
This means that if the robot drives up a hill and reaches the maximum defined eleva-
tion value, everything on the costmap becomes a lethal obstacle, as values above the
maximum are clipped. Lowering the minimum and increasing the maximum does not
result in a better outcome, as now lethal obstacles are not labeled as lethal anymore
due to the increased thresholds. For this reason, the authors describe a different
approach: The grid map library features different kinds of filters to convert the data
of the map. The authors use slopes, edges, and roughness layers combined to create
a traversability map. This process will be described later in Section 2.1.10.

2.1.8 2D Costmaps
To be able to plan a path using e.g A∗, there should be at least an environmental
representation, which is either pre-existing or recorded on-the-fly. Perception should
include at least obstacle detection, optionally, with the ability to detect dynamic
obstacles.
The most widely used method of path planning is planning on a 2D costmap [29]. In
this representation, an occupancy grid is created, and its cells are given a cost, which
often represents the probability of an obstacle occupying that cell. This probabilistic
representation helps mitigate the fact that no sensor is perfect and every measurement
has at least some noise. Thus, if an obstacle is measured to be in front of the robot, its
actual outline is inflated either with the same lethal cost or with some gradient, which
in turn results in a smooth increase of probability before reaching the maximum at
the obstacle. In ROS [26], the basic navigation framework [30] uses a library called
2D costmap. This representation uses a grayscale-value grid with each cell having a
value between 0 and 255, with 0 representing free space.
Figure 2.12: The gradient of an obstacle cost plotted against the inflation radius [29].
This so called ”inflation“ mitigates the problem with sensor noise and robot foot-
print by taking the measurement at a given point and inflating it’s radius on the map
by some distance. This can also be a falling gradient of the cost like shown in Figure
2.12. It also helps with the uncertainty of other data, such as odometry, by allowing
some tolerance in the actual driven path of the robot. Tuning the inflation is crucial
in the case of 2D navigation to avoid unnecessary excursions while making sure to
reliably avoid lethal obstacles. Another use case of inflation is the reduction of com-
putational requirements by inflating the map enough such that the robot model can
be a simple point on the map instead of the full footprint, which avoids intersection
calculations. An example for a typical inflated costmap is shown in Figure 2.13.
22
--- PAGE 32 ---
Figure 2.13: A typical inflated costmap. Black parts are lethal obstacles, while dark gray
parts are part of the inflation
While costmaps, as the name suggests, can contain the cost of traversal, they are
most of the time used to represent objects in a binary way: lethal or non-lethal/safe
to traverse. This is enough in most simple cases, and lots of robotics solutions indoors
and even outdoors use this type of method. However, these maps can be used to store
more data as will be described in Section 2.1.10. It is also important to mention, that
most of the time multiple costmaps are used in a navigation map, one static map as a
global representation of the already known or already mapped environment, and one
smaller, rolling-window type of map which moves with the robot and records dynamic
obstacles. This approach is typically used due to the constrained processing power
and the limited reliable range of the sensors. A smaller map around the robot can be
processed quickly, and also sensors only deliver data within their dedicated range.
These kind of maps are typically used with 2D-Lidars, but clever algorithms and
filters can be used to represent features of 3D terrain on them.
2.1.9 Octo maps
Expanding the dimensionality from 2D to 3D isn’t as easy as it sounds. The most
trivial method is, however, to expand the previously shown 2D grid with rectangles
to 3D boxes (or pixels) in space. These three-dimensional boxes are called voxels [31]
shown in Figure 2.14. They are widely used in video games and rendering 3D art, but
not as much in mobile robotics, due to their resource-demanding nature. Such 3D
representations is commonly used with drones, and industrial robotic arms, as they
must be aware of their full 3D surroundings to be able to avoid obstacles.
23
--- PAGE 33 ---
Figure 2.14: Voxel representation of 3D-space illustrated in a sketch [31].
A popular implementation of voxel grids is called OctoMap [32]. The authors of
OctoMap take the idea of voxels and make it much more efficient by making the map
variable in resolution using octrees [33]. Octrees are a type of data structure where
each node has exactly 8 children. They can be used to subdivide space recursively
which makes them very suitable for this kind of high dimensional problem.
If all children of a node have the same value, they can be reduced to a single node,
which leads to substantial reduction in points te be tracked in the tree as opposed to
an explicit voxel representation. The basic structure of an octree is shown in Figure
2.15.
Figure 2.15: The basic octree structure shown in a sketch [34].
This pruning process can also be intentionally done to reduce resolution which
looks as shown in Figure 2.16.

Figure 2.16: A tree represented with different resolutions showing the effect of subdivision

However, it isn’t by accident, that there isn’t much research for unmanned ground
vehicles using OctoMaps. This representation, while very efficient, isn’t really nec-
essary most of the time. A full 3D perception of the environment is only important
if every part of said environment is relevant to the problem. This is the case with
UA Vs(Unmanned aerial vehicles). UA Vs rely heavily on OctoMaps nowadays, and
there’s also a lot of research in this area regarding planning in 3D space and object
avoidance. With that being said, there are other, more efficient ways to represent the
environment, if the vehicle is bound to and constrained by the ground.
2.1.10 Grid maps and elevation mapping
Elevation mapping by F ankhauser et al.
In the paper titled ”Probabilistic Terrain Mapping for Mobile Robots With Uncertain
Localization“ [8] from Fankhauser et al. is an improvement of the unique approach
”Robot-Centric Elevation Mapping with Uncertainty Estimates“[35] from the same
authors is presented. The authors approach the problem of perceiving the world in
3D in a unique way, tackling some of the problems with previous approaches. The
most important challenge which they propose a solution to is the reliance on abso-
lute localization in existing approaches. These methods only work reliably in either
laboratory environments or with very precise tracking techniques. Fankhauser et al.
propose a novel mapping method which relies on kinematic and inertial measurements
only. Their method incorporates drift and uncertainties of the state estimation and
even the noise model of the sensor used into the map. This results in a probabilistic
estimate of the terrain where the confidence values of each cell are known and can be
taken into account any time.
The author’s approach to mapping and using grid maps is, at the time of writing,
arguably the state-of-the-art solution for 2.5D mapping for ground vehicles. While
it is important related research, the extensiveness of the explanation of the mapping
process itself would be largely out-of-scope for this thesis, and thus it will be de-
scribed in a compressed way, only handling the most relevant parts to show, how this
technique can be used in real time to create grid maps.

Grid maps
The representation that Fankhauser et al. use for their elevation mapping pipeline is
called grid maps [39], which they also develop at the company ANYbotics [11].
29
--- PAGE 39 ---
Figure 2.20: Example of a grid map used as an elevation map visualized in R Viz [39].
As this library is made explicitly for the robot-centric elevation mapping, it is
designed to move with the robot. It employs a rolling buffer for this, which is very
efficient. The library allows for different layers to be stored in their custom ROS-
message. For the author’s standard elevation mapping library typically a raw layer
and a fused layer are used, where the first one can be employed to monitor the mapping
process, while the second one is the computed and fused elevation map as described in
the previous Section. The map update and fusion are multi-threaded and thus work
very fast. The authors also propose a visualization library for the application R Viz
[40], which is a data visualizer specifically made for ROS. An example visualization
can be seen in Figure 2.20. A grid map uses a set of 2D coordinates represented
as the cells. The difference from the classic 2D costmap is that 2D costmapts are
only able to represent an integer cell value from 0 to 255, while a grid map can save
an arbitrary floating point value in each cell. This allows for a decent 2.5D surface
representation, depending on the resolution.
The most relevant feature of the library for this thesis is, that it allows to use
a variety of filters, and even chain them together. Filters include inpainting-filter,
which fills in holes where no data is available, normal vector filter, slope filter, and
even custom mathematical expressions for custom filtering. Custom filters can in-
clude any formula which can be applied per cell or as a kernel over the entire map.
Some approaches in path planning exploit this feature to create traversability maps
using some risk metric calculated using such filters. Filtered maps can be saved in
additional layers and accessed by layer name and cell position. Fankhauser et al.
also demonstrate this employing such a traversability filter in their demo library for
elevation mapping. The library also provides a C++ interface which can be used to
do further processing and data handling on grid maps. Most importantly, grid maps
can be directly converted (or mapped) to a 2D costmap representation using a single
function. The converter can use any layer from a filter chain, and it is also possible
to combine layers in a custom way, which makes this a very powerful approach with
lots of possibilities. Due to it being written in C++ the conversion is very efficient,
without any noticeable performance overhead, and can be used to do multiple con-
versions per second in real time.
Although the grid map library is the most practical way of representing 2.5D ter-
30
--- PAGE 40 ---
rain, it also has a drawback, namely the lack of ability to represent overhangs and
tunnels. Proposed methods like presented in [41] exist to solve this problem, but
they are yet to be incorporated into the library. The main solution to this problem
is to restrict the maximum mapped height, which allows the robot to pass under an
overhang ( like a bridge), but not over it, as it isn’t mapped anymore.
Grid maps and elevation mapping are arguably the most efficient and robust method
to represent terrain at the time of writing and thus they will also be used in this
thesis to represent the environment.
2.2 Research of path planning pipelines
The presented algorithms and mapping methods are widely used in real robots. How-
ever, most solutions rely on a well-tuned 2D obstacle map where lethal and non-lethal
obstacles are defined to cover the worst-case scenarios and allow for safe traversal.
These solutions are computationally efficient, however, they lack a more complex un-
derstanding of the environment which often leads to missed opportunities in path
planning, more energy consumption, and longer paths, while also potentially ignoring
dangerous situations. Researchers are thus working on more advanced ways of path
planning by designing complete proprietary pipelines and systems from perception
and mapping to control. These systems are developed by large teams, each with a
unique approach, building the system from the ground up and integrating the plan-
ning and mapping process in a way that takes rough terrain into account. These
works mostly focus on a specific robotic hardware, taking the precise kinematics of
the given research platform into account and planning according to its limits.
2.2.1 ArtPlanner
ArtPlanner is one of the few planning systems which uses native 3D data to plan
and takes detailed kinematic properties of the robot into account. The pipeline uses
an earlier version of the previously described elevation mapping [27], which is partly
confirmed by the fact, that one of the authors, Marco Hutter has also worked on
the elevation mapping method at the ETH Z ¨urich, but the authors don’t explicitly
mention the library.
Using strict kinematic constraints for planning is also the greatest disadvantage of
this pipeline, because it has been specifically created for the ANYmal robot from
ANYbotics [11] shown in Figure 2.21, and cannot be used for any other robot with-
out heavy modifications.
31
--- PAGE 41 ---
Figure 2.21: The ANYmal C on the left, which ArtPlanner was designed for, and its successor,
the ANYmal D on the right [11].
The art planner works like the sampling-based method called LazyPRM ∗ (a vari-
ant of probabilistic roadmaps) which could not be handled in this thesis due to time
constraints. LazyPRM ∗ works similarly to RRT shown in Section 2.1.6, with improve-
ments in efficiency and speed, which is important in case of this planner, due to the
larger number of calculations needed for each sample. The authors combine sampling
with a reachability check using the robot’s kinematics. The basic assumption they
make is that the robot’s torso must stay contact-free with the environment, while the
legs must always be able to have contact. They do this by abstracting the legs and
torso into collision boxes and check for collisions at each sampled point. Figure 2.22
shows this abstraction, along with the decomposed parts of the world, with (b) being
the part which must not come in contact with the torso, and (c) which must have
contact with the legs:

Additionally, the authors have trained a convolutional neural network (CNN) to
compute a foothold score based on the height map and use this score to reduce the
regions considered for valid footholds. They also introduce a learned motion cost,
which is necessary to avoid the robot stepping close to obstacles or edges, which
would otherwise be possible just based on the reachability map. They again use
a CNN to combine the energy, time and risk associated with moving from a given
position to the goal. They train this network using simulated synthetic data with

As can be seen in Figure 2.23 the algorithm first ”lazily“ samples the space and

be used to find the path based on cost.
The authors also handle a relevant question of uncertainty when planning in caves,
and in case of negative obstacles. They use virtual surfaces on areas not physically
visible to the LIDAR sensor, which works well going uphill, however it introduces a
false sense of security in case of steep negative obstacles. At unknown spaces if a
ray is cast between the maximum height and the last known surface part, a virtual
surface is created instead of using some in-painting algorithm. This virtual surface

and the last known surface. Figure 2.24 from the authors illustrates this in a sketch:

At the end the authors describe some issues with this pipeline. One of the issues
regarding negative obstacles and virtual surfaces has already been explained. An-
other problem using the height map was at the opening of the cave where they tested
the solution. Up to 1 meter before the opening the robot had a so-called ”halluci-

after the opening, similarly like in Figure 2.24, Subfigure 3. The robot was still able
to plan further, but had to stop a few times for the map to update. After entering the
cave, this hallucinated wall then appeared behind the robot which would have made
exiting just as hard as entering. Last but not least, the authors describe the used
path follower, Pure Pursuit. This algorithm performed well tracking paths precisely
however the authors have noticed a non-constant delay between receiving a path and
the controller starting to follow it, which made the robot get stuck in risky areas.
Due to these issues the authors acknowledge the complexity of such a pipeline built
up from multiple components, where every component relies on the other.
2.2.2 Traversability-Based RRT ∗ [12]
The authors of this paper, Takemurea et al. use a similar approach as ArtPlanner,
but with much less complexity which is partially due to the fact, that it’s designed
for a wheeled rover instead of a legged robot. They however take a different approach
and basically don’t bother mapping the environment in the classical way at all, but
rather use the raw 3D point cloud which they get either from a depth camera or
the 3D LIDAR of the rover. They also acknowledge this at the beginning of the
paper, and describe other methods which use some kind of traversability or height
map. As opposed to grid-based methods sampling-based methods can be used in
non-continuous and high dimensional spaces. These properties are important for the
presented method, as a raw pointcloud doesn’t have a uniform density over the whole
dataset, due to the natural dispersion of light coming from the LIDAR, or going into
the depth camera. However, a random sampling algorithm is unaware of this incon-
sistency.
Due to the converging optimality of RRT ∗, the authors chose this algorithm as the
basis of their work and improved it by introducing additional costs for terrain aware-
ness. As the RRT ∗ algorithm has already been described in Section 2.1.6 only the

2.2.3 Risk-Aware Mapping and Planning
The authors of the paper ”Risk-Aware Mapping and Planning“[13], Sharma et al.
approach the problem of off-road traversal in a way which is usable in real-time and
basically improves known tools and algorithms to better understand traversability of
known and unknown terrain.
The problem the authors describe is the lack of awareness of known and unknown
spaces in the control phase. Most approaches do not take unknown space into account,
and treat it as free space on a costmap which might lead to dangerous situations.
To add to this problem, this unknown space might have been known before, but is
overwritten due to LIDAR-shadow when the robot is at an incline and can’t see the
ground or obstacle anymore. This problem is illustrated in Figurte 2.28.

The authors discuss a possible solution, which is penalizing speed in unknown
space, which is a known approach to solving this problem, however, other works do
not incentivize moving faster in free space, which often leads to slow average speeds.
They begin with briefly describing their pipeline and contributions to 3D-aware plan-
ning which tries to solves the described problems. The authors introduce a 3D-aware
2D-map representation with memory based on LIDAR point inflation, which can accu-
rately assess 3D occupancy data relevant for driving, while retaining computational
advantage due to it’s 2D structure. They also introduce a custom MPPI (Model-
predictive-path-integral) controller which takes variable time-horizons into account
to enable high-speed traversal in known space, while keeping speed adjustable based

2.3.1 Physics engines
Physics engines are the heart of a robotics simulator. The better the physics of the
simulated scenario the more accurate and true-to-life are the results. In robotics, the
most constraining factor of any task is always the kinematics and capabilities of the
given robot. Highly accurate physics allows for tighter constraints and less tolerance
in the configuration of the algorithms and realistic representation of contact and in-
teraction with the terrain.
The most popular physics engines for robotics include:
• Bullet [45]
• MuJoCo [46]
• ODE [47]
• DART [48]
• Raisim [49]
Choosing the best one from the list isn’t straightforward. All of these engines have
their advantages and disadvantages, but more importantly, they each have their spe-
cific purpose.
The Legged Robotics Laboratory at the ETH Z ¨urich developed RaiSim [49] for the
specific purpose of training their legged robots using reinforcement learning. Natu-
rally, the authors of RaiSim, Kang et al., have done multiple tests and comparisons
[50] on their physics engine, which will be considered in this thesis. Using RaiSim
itself is not a viable option for this thesis because it’s not designed for real-time sim-
ulation but rather repeated tests in a controlled environment to train legged robots.
During testing it was determined, that writing a custom interface between Raisim
and ROS is not feasible due to time constraints. However, the comparison provided
by the authors is still very valuable, and the choice of the physics engine for this
thesis will be based on their article.

The authors have tested multiple important aspects of the physics engines. The
following aspects are the most important for the context of this thesis:
• Rolling: To test rolling, Kang et al. use 25 1kg spheres rolling on a surface,
where the surface and the spheres are given a different friction coefficient. They
evaluate the metric by comparing the velocity of the objects in the simulation
with an analytical solution
• Bouncing: For testing bounce the authors drop 7x7 10Kg perfectly elastic
spheres with zero to the static ground. The results are compared by measuring
the system energy and comparing it to an analytic solution
• Hard contact (666 test): For testing hard contact the authors use a standardized
test of a 6x6x6 cube of 10Kg perfectly inelastic spheres with zero friction. The
pairwise penetration of spheres is measured, which indicates the error in the
simulation
• Elasticity (Elastic 666): To test elasticity the authors use the elastic version of
the 666 test, consisting of a 6x6x6 cube of 10Kg perfectly elastic (restitutional
coefficient of 1) spheres with zero friction. The system energy is measured and
compared to an analytic solution like in the case of the bouncing test
As the authors mention, solving contact dynamics is an NP-hard problem due to its
discontinuity. For this reason, the physics engine solves the contact problem in a
relaxed way using efficient solvers. These solvers each use different methods making
them better or worse at certain tasks. Some engines sacrifice the accuracy of certain
calculations to speed up the solution, which leads to poor results in some scenarios.
Going into more detail about physics calculations would be largely out of scope, which
is why the choice will rely on the comparison results of the authors.

2.4 Terrain generation
For accurate off-road simulation, a true-to-life terrain with high variability is just as
important as the physics engine or the simulator itself. Although there are multiple
ways to generate terrain, even by hand, a random, close-to-life method is much better
to ensure real-world scenarios in simulation.
49
--- PAGE 59 ---
2.4.1 Perlin Noise
Perlin noise [53] is a type of noise function developed in 1983 by Ken Perlin and had
the original purpose to make computer-generated graphics look more realistic. Soon
it was discovered, that this type of noise can also be used for much more, such as tex-
ture generation, pseudo-random noise, and generating clouds and surfaces. The latter
is the use case, that is mostly taken advantage of in the robotics community. To name
an example, a traversability study from Getahun et al. titled ”Local traversability
assessment in an unmanned ground vehicle“ made with the Husky used Perlin noise
to generate the test terrains for the robot and used elevation mapping to map the ter-
rains. In another paper [54] from Miki et al. from the ETH Z ¨urich Robotic Systems
Lab, the authors train the ANYmal [11] shown in Section 2.2.1 using reinforcement
learning on terrains generated using Perlin noise, with the goal of improving locomo-
tion on rough terrain. Due to its success in other literature, Perlin noise will be used
to generate the terrains for this thesis.
2.4.2 Using Blender for terrain generation
While there are much easier methods to generating terrain, using Blender is both quick
and a multi-purpose solution. Generating the terrains in Blender has the positive side-
effect of being able to export the terrain as both a ground-truth pointcloud for the
elevation mapping and as an object file (.obj) for CoppeliaSim. The terrains also can
be easily and quickly modified at any time with simple functions. The works [55] and
[54] also employ Blender to generate uneven surfaces.
Blender also has built-in features for random terrain generation. One of these features
is the ability to use and apply noise textures to surfaces [56]. Noise textures in Blender
all use Perlin noise as a basis, and can be chained together with other modifiers to e.g.
change the magnitude of the terrain or apply additional noise at another frequency
to it.
2.5 Conclusions of research
Looking at the available state-of-the-art research on 2.5D planning, it is evident, that
there’s no single ”best practice“ or general solution available. The most generaliz-
able approaches to this day still use 2D costmaps at some point in the pipeline, and
plan using a basic algorithm like A ∗. STEP, as described in Section 2.2.4 uses A ∗
on a well-prepared 2D map to find the shortest path on sections that have already
been labeled as traversable. As seen in Sections 2.2.2, 2.2.1, 2.2.4, and 2.2.3 solu-
tions are usually developed for specific problems and specific hardware/robots, rather
than proposing a general method. At the time of writing, there isn’t any general so-
lution for rough, 2.5D terrain-based path planning using native 2.5D data, which
would work in any scenario and on different hardware. There are methods like the
STEP-pipeline described in Section 2.2.4 which generalizes well to different robots
50
--- PAGE 60 ---
and scenarios, however, it is a very complex, and closed-source system with many
components relying on each other. RAMP [13] described in Section 2.2.3 takes a
step in the direction of a more general solution, by putting more emphasis on under-
standing and modeling the environment, and defining a problem that is prominent in
off-road scenarios. The authors focus their efforts on the problem of LIDAR shadows
and the motion on slopes to which they propose a controller-based solution. Their
work only uses known algorithms and methods (like converting a 2.5D elevation map
to a 2D costmap) and their contribution extends the model-predictive control with
additional constraints, which generalizes well in off-road environments, and could be
used on different hardware with minimal re-tuning.
The ArtPlanner presented in Section 2.2.1 proposes a well-working solution using 2.5D
data natively, but heavily relies on the ANYmal which is a very capable quadruped
robot with a closed-source and reinforcement-learning-based control loop, making it
hard to evaluate how effective or useful the method of planning for wheeled or tracked
robots really is. Nonetheless, their efforts show, that an efficient, sampling-based
planning algorithm can be effectively extended with complex kinematic constraints
for real-time planning. Finally, Traversability-Based RRT∗ presented in Section 2.2.2
attempts to use as much of the information about the environment as possible by
planning natively on the gathered pointcloud, thus using 2.5D terrain data with-
out any pre-processing. Similarly to the ArtPlanner, the authors demonstrate, that
a sampling-based algorithm serves as a good basis in the presence of constraints,
achieving good performance and good quality paths.

3.2.1 Systems for Path Planning and Execution for Off-Road Envi-
ronments
In order to evaluate the quality of a path produced by the path planners a complete
system of perception, planning and execution is needed, as the robot will be execut-
ing the plans in the simulation system. The building blocks of this system will be
described in this section.
2.5D grid map and conversion to 2D
As determined during research, a grid map presented in Section 2.1.10 provides the
most efficient and state-of-the-art representation for 2.5D terrains. This representa-
tion is also supported by elevation mapping, which provides the necessary environ-
ment perception and also tackles challenges with sensor noise and odometry drift.
Although mapping isn’t directly used in this work, it is an essential element of future
work and real-life tests. We will use ground truth elevation maps in the evaluation
in order to not introduce a bias by the mapping approach. The papers [13], [10], and
[14] all in some way use a version of 2.5D elvation maps for their cost estimations.
Thus the proposed methods will also use elevation maps using the grid map library
[39], due to their efficiency and wide range of features.
The first method, which follows the style of papers [14] and [13] will implement
some conversion mechanism, that filters the 2.5D grid map and converts it into a 2D
costmap while maintaining terrain awareness. This will be done using a slope filter,
which converts slope data into cost values on the 2D costmap. This method works
by applying a mathematical filter to the elevation map, which calculated the normal
vector of every cell on the map, and takes the arccosine of these normal vectors. This
results in a map of slope values in radians. By tuning the maximum and minimum
range of the cells values the map can be adjusted to fit the kinematic abilities of the
robot. It is known from the datasheet of the Husky, that its maximum claimable slope
is 30 degrees. It is known from initial testing, that the realistically climable slope on
a rocky surface is around 25 degrees. By adjusting the maximum degree of slope to
be 27 degrees, any slope that’s over this value will be clipped to the maximum lethal
value on the map. This results in a gradient of slope values between 0 and 27 degrees,
which is a good representation of where the robot can safely traverse. The cost of a
cell on tha map thus looks as follows:
C(x, y) = min(255 · α(x, y)
27 ) (3.1)
where α represents the calculated slope value in degrees at given map coordinates.
The second method will use the height and terrain data of the 2.5D grid map
natively during planning, similarly to the works in [10], [12] and [14] by using the
robot’s footprint to determine posture.

Grid maps are usually used in a robot-centric way with elevation mapping, how-
ever as the terrains are fixed, it will be used in a static way in this thesis, as all the
data is known as a ground-truth point cloud directly exported from Blender. The
main limitations of map size are the file size for storage and the processing power
needed to use them. The maps used should be as large as it is practically possible
with 50m x 50m being the target (determined by previous experience). This means,
that the tested algorithms are assumed to work seamlessly on a real setup, using a
smaller, rolling map (typically 10m x 10m to 20m x 20m max due to sensor range
limitations), if they are able to achieve good enough performance on such a large map.
By using grid maps it is also possible to define custom mathematical functions as
filters if needed, which can potentially be used for more advanced traversability anal-
ysis in future work. As the generated terrains are smooth, such traversability filters
in addition to the slope values would not make any difference. However it is planned
for future work to generate more detailed, rough surfaces, where traversability filters
such as for roughness could also be tested. For this work it was more important to
test such a 2D compressed slope-map in a simple way, so that it generalizes well, and
the comparisons stay fair.
Search algorithms on a converted 2D costmap
The algorithms presented in Section 2.1.2 - with the exception of the default A ∗ of
ROS - all only use collision checks in their basic implementations. Thus they will
be slightly modified to consider the cost on a 2D costmap in addition to the cost
calculated from the path length. The magnitude of the cost should be configurable
by dynamic reconfiguration, to be able to quickly determine a suitable scaling factor
without recompiling the code every time. It is important, that the default function-
ality and cost functions should still work as intended and described in Section 2.1.2.
The algorithms which will be used for standard planning on a 2D map are A∗, Theta∗,
D∗, and RRT ∗. Normal RRT will not be considered, as it has no benefit compared
to RRT∗, and is considered obsolete. As the proposed, extended method developed
for he evaluation using 2.5D information naively also uses RRT ∗ as its basis. This
will allow for a fair comparison to see any advantage or disadvantage of using native
height data. The algorithms Theta ∗, and D ∗ are expected to perform similarly to
A∗, because the underlying heuristics guarantee an optimal solution for each of these
algorithms. Theta ∗ might perform worse than A ∗, due to it connecting line-of-sight
nodes without considering the costs between them, while D ∗ is expected to find simi-
lar paths to A ∗ but with shorter planning time. Although D ∗ is expected to produce
nearly the same paths as A ∗, it will be considered during testing to see if any disad-
vantage in planning time arises. If D ∗ performs at least as good as A ∗ considering
planning time, it means, that D ∗ could be a better candidate for real-life tests where
frequent re-planning is required, as D ∗ doesn’t have to re-iterate the whole space for

--- PAGE 65 ---
a new plan, as described in Section 2.1.4.
The cost function of each algorithm including heuristics than looks as follows:
C(n) = g(n) + h(n) + γ(n) (3.2)
Where γ(n) represents the cost of the cell at position n. C(n) is than the estimated
cost of the path from the start node until the goal, taking node n. The cost g(n)
is the already accumulated cost until the last node before expansion, and h(n) is
the heuristic cost, which in the case of A ∗ is the Euclidean distance to the goal.
All algorithms will try to minimize the cost C(n) for their path. While it isn’t a
mathematically complex extension, it works well on a 2D costmap representation. It
would however not have any influence on an occupancy grid map, where only lethal
and non-lethal costs are defined without any gradient transition between them. In
this case, these algorithms would work as the original implementation, by avoiding
lethal obstacles while planning the shortest path possible.
Extended RR T∗
Similarly to the approaches presented in [10] and [12] a method shall be developed
which can use the available 2.5D data natively to its advantage. Converting a 2.5D
map into a 2D always requires some way of compression, which will lead to loss of
information. This is fine in some scenarios, where not all the information is relevant
anyway, and in cases where limited computing power is available. But it can be
disadvantageous on complex terrain, leading to not being able to plan in scenarios,
where it would otherwise be possible with better knowledge of the terrain.
The most important factor when planning on rough terrain is the locomotion ca-
pabilities of the robot. This is strictly bounded by its kinematic limits, like tipping
angle, and max climbing angle. Following the idea of [12] RRT ∗ will be extended
with an additional cost, which projects the robot’s footprint onto the 2.5D data and
determines a cost from its attitude. Instead of doing this on a pointcloud, it will be
done on a 2.5D grid map, practically mixing some of the ideas from [10] and [12].
This has the advantage, that the solution will be usable with a real-time mapping
solution (elevation mapping described in Section 2.1.10), and keeps filtering options
and easy manipulation of the map using grid map filters open for future work. The
choice of RRT∗ ensures, that given enough time and samples, the solution should con-
verge to an optimal path. The choice of RRT ∗ is practical, because a sampling-based
solution is not only efficient, but easy to extend with additional constraints. It is
however planned for future work to also test oder sampling-based methods as a basis
for this constribution, like LazyPRM∗, that is also used by the ArtPlanner 2.2.1. It is
expected, that extending the algorithm with multiple computations at every sample
and rewire step will have some impact on performance. Depending on the gravity of
this impact future work will be outlined to make it more efficient. Thus, the proposed
56
--- PAGE 66 ---
method of cost calculation should be portable and not dependent on RRT ∗.
The planner will still technically sample a 2D map, but use the 2.5D information
for the cost estimation. As explained in Section 3.1 a 2.5D grid map is a representa-
tion of points in a 2D space ( R2) with an additional height component for each. By
projecting the robot’s footprint onto this grid at any given point, the representation
of the robot’s position and attitude (a rigid body in 3D space) can be described with
the SE(3) representation [59] of the robot’s possible translation and attitude in space.
The projected plane (called pseudo-plane in [12], from Section 2.2.2) has a normal
vector orthogonal to the surface of the plane at an arbitrary sampling point, which
at any given time is solely dependent on the plane’s position relative to the grid map.
From this normal vector, the roll and pitch values can be extracted (similarly to [14],
from Section 2.2.4) and used in a cost function. The cost function can be a weighted
sum of the values as shown in [12] from Section 2.2.2. The yaw or rotation of the
currently sampled point on the SE(2)(+height) plane is dependent on the previous
(parent) node in case of RRT∗, and is calculated by taking the angle between the par-
ent and currently sampled node in reference to the map’s frame. Thus by knowing
the SE(2) translation and rotation of the sample the SE(3) position and orientation
can also be calculated on the 2.5D map. The angle could also be used to restrict
the direction of tree growth for RRT ∗ similarly to [12] described in Section 2.2.2, but
it has been decided against due to time constraints, and due to the fact that this
would require modifying RRT ∗ itself, binding the solution to this specific algorithm.
Nonetheless, it can be extended by this additional cost in future work, if RRT ∗ is
concluded to be a good basis algorithm for this method.
To make this implementation as flexible as possible the 2.5D elevation map will be
used instead of a raw pointcloud. The authors of [12] don’t actually use the pseudo
plane directly, but rather chose the maximum angle of the four wheels at any given
point. This is fine for a rocker-bogie-style rover, however, it isn’t a good representa-
tion for a rigid platform like the Husky. Instead, the normal vector of the projected
plane will be used to calculate the roll and pitch angles similarly to the technique
used in STEP [14].
The four wheels of the robot form a rectangle. Projecting a rigid rectangle onto
a height map is not a trivial problem. If two opposing corners of a rectangle are at
different heights it leads to a diagonal split in the middle, which separates the rectan-
gle into 2 triangles. The problem is, that now there are two possible normal vectors,
one for each half, without any obvious method to choose one. Figure 3.1 illustrates
this issue.

Controller
Selecting a suitable controller for path execution wasn’t a concern of this thesis,
however it is still needed to follow the planned path. This means, that the controller
should follow the paths as strictly as possible, such that the influence of it on the
end result is minimized. Keeping this in mind, and taking the example of the paper
STEP [14] described in Section 2.2.4 and RAMP [13] described in Section 2.2.3,
an MPC(Model predictive control)-based local planner, namely TEB (Timed Elastic
Bands [61]) has been selected. This local planner will be using the parameters of the
robot, including max speed and wheelbase, and set up to follow the path as strictly
as possible by adjusting the control weights. Using a simple proportional-integral-
based controller like the popular Stanley-controller [62] was not an option, as the
planned trajectories might not be kinematically feasible (especially in the case of the
graph-based algorithms), and model-aware control is needed. Using an MPC-based
controller keeps the option of extending the controller to be terrain-aware similarly
to STEP [14] and RAMP [13] open in future work. TEB could also be configured
to avoid dynamic obstacles, which might be necessary in the future if the proposed
methods aren’t capable of fast re-planning.
3.2.2 Design of evaluation
Evaluatuion scenarios
To gather enough data the algorithms have to be tested on multiple different terrains.
As test scenarios 6 different terrains were generated using Blender, 5 of them with
its built-in noise texture node using Perlin noise as a basis. The terrains vary in
difficulty and type by due to adjusting the magnitude, frequency, and gain of the
noise texture randomly. All 5 of these terrains will be tried from two different start
positions, with 5 goals each. Choosing opposite corners on each of the maps results in
a completely different problem from the perspective of the algorithms, which doubles
the number of unique problems for each of the 5 terrains. It was not feasible to
generate more terrains for this thesis due to the time it takes to test one terrain and
the number of recorded data points, however, it is left for future work to automate
the generation process as well and test an arbitrary number of terrains any time.
The 6th terrain was created manually, specifically to demonstrate the advantage of
the extended method more clearly. A terrain with higher magnitude and variation is
considered more difficult. On more difficult terrains, simpler algorithms are expected
to produce longer paths, more energy consumption (due to slippage of the robot
or unideal traversal points), and longer traversal time. The goal positions on these
terrains have been defined empirically such that redundant paths are avoided as much
as possible, while still enough data is gathered. The robot always starts at one corner
of a map to ensure that as much area on the map is covered as possible.

Evaluation metrics
A carefully designed methodology is needed for a suitable comparison of path plan-
ning methods. As this work targets off-road planning specifically, the evaluation
metrics should be designed accordingly. The most problematic property of off-road
traversal is the fact, that uneven terrain easily pushes the hardware to its limits. This
means, that finding routes that don’t exhaust the robot’s capabilities to a dangerous
level while keeping the path reasonably short and quickly traversable are the most
important goals of such a path planner.
While dynamic obstacles will not be tested due to time constraints, it is still impor-
tant, that the planner doesn’t take long to provide a solution, to enable re-planning
in case of more dynamic environments, as this is a requirement of future work.
We developed a set of metrics for our evaluation of path planning algorithms for off
road environments:
• standard deviations of roll and pitch are expected to be lower for the
2D-based algorithms, as these will try to minimize the cost on the slope-based
costmap. A path with less standard deviation of roll and pitch is considered
smoother, but not necessarily better, as choosing flatter sections might produce
significantly longer paths.
• the length of the planned path can be estimated from 2D coordinates
returned by the algorithms by summing up the sections from start to the goal.
• traveled path length calculated using the odometry is expected to devi-
ate from the generated path due to slippage and sharp turns, but also due to the
fact, that coarser sections are used to calculate the 2D path length, which the
robot will not always be able to follow with 100% accuracy. If traveled length
and the generated length deviate by a large margin it might indicate slippage
due to low path quality.
• required energy to complete a path gives a good indication of planning qual-
ity. Paths with similar lengths produced by different algorithms are expected
to deviate in energy consumption. 4-wheeled differential-drive robots are also
known to require more energy while turning due to friction.
• average of torque of the robot jointsindicate the average of the momentary
efforts the robot had to make along the path. This metric is expected to indicate
how kinematically feasible the planned path is. It is expected to be higher for
more aggressive plans, due to more changes in the terrain during the execution.
• travel time from sending the goal to reaching the goal is expected to be
correlated with path length, however it might deviate due to slippage if a low
quality path is provided which is harder to travel along.
61
--- PAGE 71 ---
• planning time is the time from sending the goal to getting a path back from
the planner, which is an important metric for future work, where frequent re-
planning might be needed.
• successful traversal of the generated path gives an indication if the planner
has provided an traversable path. This metric will be constrained by time. If
the robot doesn’t reach the goal position in a certain maximum time frame, it
can be assumed, that it has gotten stuck due to a low quality path.

The tests will be conducted for A ∗, Theta ∗, D ∗, RRT∗ and the proposed, extended
RRT∗. For every algorithm the program iterates through the 5 generated terrains,

As no good automated way of finding goal positions could be determined, they have
been chosen empirically by driving the robot manually on the map and verifying
reachability. This process also ensures that the goals are actually physically achiev-

4.1.1 Setup of the height map and conversion method
For the implementation of the backbone of this work, the standard, openly avail-
able, and maintained library elevation mapping [27] and grid maps [39] developed by
Fankhauser et al. have been used.
The elevation mapping library only requires a few prerequisites to be installed, includ-
ing the very efficient Eigen library [64] for linear algebra operations. It is to mention
that strictly speaking no mapping library would be required for this work in theory,
as all the data already exists in a singular pointcloud file as ground truth. This means
that the values of these points could be converted to a grid map representation with
e.g. a custom Python script that publishes the data using the ROS Python interface.
In the case of this thesis, the elevation mapping library acts as a converter, and its
advanced uncertainty-aware features described in Section 2.1.10 are not used. The
reason for the decision to keep the elevation mapping in the pipeline was, that the
system will be used with the same stack on a real robot, and thus only some minimal
configuration would be needed to test the presented methods in real life as well. It is
also a very efficient library, and no performance overhead was noticeable by its usage.
The installation is as simple as pulling the git repository into the catkin workspace
and compiling using catkin. The package already contains the necessary makefiles.
The package comes with a demo folder made by the authors, which provides a great
template for setup and even includes different sensor configurations for sensor noise,
mentioned in Section 2.1.10. There are a lot of different configurations that have to
be set precisely in order for the library to work properly. First and foremost the setup
of the map:

In line 2 the first filter defined is the inpaint filter. Inpainting is originally used in
the field of computer vision to fill holes on a raster or picture, but can also be used to
fill grid cells if there’s no data available at a given point. In the case of this thesis, this
would not be needed, because the pointcloud is exported as a ground truth with per-
fect spacing and density. Nonetheless, it was kept in to show the configuration, that
will be used later on a real robot. In line 8 an average radius filter is applied, which
smooths out rough places on the map. This filter again does not change anything in
the case of the tests conducted, since the exported pointclouds form a smooth and
uniform surface. In real life, the radius of this filter depends on the quality of the
pointcloud. From experience usually a value between 2 to 2,5 times the cell size pro-
duces the best results. Next, on line 14 perhaps the most important filter the normal
vectors filter is defined. This filter calculates the normal vector for a defined radius.
The radius should be left only slightly larger than the cell size to get good results, as
this waay the filter only uses the neighboring cells for the calculation. If this radius
is set to multiples (more than 2x) of the cell size, than an coarser, averaged normal
vector will be saved for that cell. This behavior is undesirable in the case of this
67
--- PAGE 77 ---
work, as the quality of this filter defines the reliability of the slope map later. The
normal vectors than can be used in a math expression filter defined on line 21 to ex-
tract a slope map by taking the arccosine of the normal vectors. This results in a map
of slope values expressed in radians. With 0 rad representing a completely flat surface.
It would be possible to also construct a ”roughness“-filter, by subtracting the smooth,
averaged map made with the mean radius filter from the original, untouched map in
a math expression filter, forming a layer with potentially rough areas and assigning
a cost to them. This layer could then be combined with the slope layer using some
weighing on each component to form a traversability map. Such a method is highly
dependent on the resolution of the map, but nonetheless, it can be used effectively
if tuned correctly. The custom cost method developed in the STEP paper [14] de-
scribed in Section 2.2.4 is similar to this approach. This however would have no effect
in the case of the generated terrains in this work, as they have perfect surfaces, which
means, that the only valuable cost information in this case are the slope angle values.
However, this is a promising feature for the detailed analysis of the terrain, and it will
be explored in future work to potentially improve the performance of the proposed
extended algorithm. Another possibility would be an edge detection filter, using the
sliding-window math expression, which is another built-in filtering type. With this
filter, it is possible to incorporate a custom mathematical expression into a sliding
window of arbitrary size, which is continuously processed on the map, like a kernel
fitler.
For the implementation of the extended method using native 2.5D data, no filters
are needed and the raw elevation map could be used as is. As the grid map library
provides all resulting outputs of the filter chain as layers, no modifications are required
to the configuration, it is as simple as subscribing to the ”elevation“ layer from line
5 instead of the slope.

The above snippet is taken from a callback function, which is called every time a new

normal grid map structure. The second function is the part that converts the ele-

imply, that the resulting map only has binary values, with cells being occupied or
not, however, this representation uses the 2D costmap format, which allows values
from 0 to 255 per cell. The function takes 4 arguments. The first one, ”map“ is
the grid map itself, which the callback function triggers to. This is always the latest
data available. The second and third variables are the minimum and maximum slope
values in radians, that the given map representation (0 - 255) will be mapped to.
This is the setting that defines the range of the costmap and is the most crucial part
of the classical planning method using 2D data, as it directly influences the behavior
of the path planning algorithm. These values must be tuned as precisely as possible
to the kinematic capabilities of the robot.
Jumping back to the slope map from earlier, it has been shown, that the slope values
are calculated using the arccosine of the normal vectors. This resulted in a map of
slope values in radians. The maximum traversable slope of the Husky is known to be

--- PAGE 79 ---
30 degrees from its datasheet. Based on previous experience and initial tests in the
simulation, the safely traversable slope (which does not take turning on slopes into
account) is around 25 degrees. If the range of values on line 2 is set to the minimum
0 and maximum to 0.47, this means that the resulting map will represent a gradient
slope map from 0 rad (completely flat) being free space, and 0.47 rad (around 28
degrees) being occupied space. Everything above 0.47 rad is clipped to the maximum
value. Setting the value to 25 degrees would result in a lot of missed opportunities on
the map, which would otherwise be traversable, as the cost would be way too high.
Setting the value higher however would result in less cost at places where the robot
could not physically traverse. 28 degrees of angle was thus determined empirically to
be the best choice, by running initial tests. It is also to be mentioned, that this value
depends on the slipperiness of the surface which is configurable in the simulation, and
has been set to the most realistic value for a hard, gravel-like terrain with uneven
surface. This will be described in more detail in Section 4.2.2. The last argument
represents the converted costmap which is given by reference. The gradient is unfor-
tunately limited by the 2D costmap’s data structure, only allowing values from 0 to
255. which limits this compressed representation even further, However for a total
number of 256 possible values for 0 to 0.47 rad this results in a resolution of about
0.002 rad (0.11 deg) per step, which is expected to be plenty of resolution for this use
case.

4.1.2 Setup and implementation of the standard 2D planners
For practical reasons, move base [30] will be used as the base navigation framework for
the algorithms. This approach has the advantage, that the algorithms under testing
can be used as plugins and switched out quickly without recompiling or reconfiguring
the code, speeding up the testing procedure. This includes both the ”standard“ plan-
ners, which will be used on the converted 2D costmap, as well as the method using
native 2.5D data.
For path planning on the previously introduced costmap, some algorithms have
already been selected to be tried in Section 2.1.2. The algorithms, A ∗, Theta ∗, D ∗,
and RRT∗ are all well-known algorithms and have been implemented multiple times.
Ai-Winter [57] provides a collection of path planning algorithms made for ROS on
their Github page, which is openly available. This library has the advantage, that the
implementations use the exact methodology as described in the original papers. The
authors made it very convenient to switch between these algorithms by providing
a wrapper for the default move base navigation framework provided by ROS. The
issue with these implementations is that they are based on the completely original
and vanila versions of the algorithms, and they only check for collisions (occupancy)
and distance heuristics by design. Thus, more cost-awareness is needed to plan on
a slope map shown in the previous section. For A ∗ the base global planner [58] im-
plementation of ROS navigation [30] can be used without further modification, as
this implementation was designed to be aware of gradient costs rather than taking
a binary collision check. The cost values distributed on a slope map are thus taken
into account, instead of just checking for collision (where the cost is above a lethal
threshold) and using distance as a heuristic. This implementation also uses quadratic
approximation, which is a type of smoothing applied to the produced path, which
71
--- PAGE 81 ---
would otherwise just follow the grid cells.
The remaining algorithms are extended by an additional cost, which is the cost of the
current cell on the costmap calculated in the previous section to function like the A ∗
implementation of ROS. This cost is applied additionally to the distance cost.
Even though a smoothing technique would likely improve performance further, it was
out-of-scope to investigate for this thesis.
To show an example, the cost calculation for A ∗ looks as follows:
1 node_new.set_g(current.g() + m.g() + costmap_->getCharMap()[
grid2Index(node_new.x(), node_new.y())] *factor_);
The above line shows the cost calculation for a new node being processed. The
current.g() function represents the cost accumulated this far, m.g() represents the
heuristic distance cost, and the last term shows the added cost from the costmap.
This makes it more costly for the algorithm to move to a cell with a steeper slope,
than to a flatter area. The cost factor has been tuned by hand, such that they produce
the least amount of undriveable paths where the robot would get stuck in a difficult
situation. This required re-testing potential scaling factors multiple times on different
terrains. As all the graph-based algorithms work like A ∗ at their basis, they calculate
the cost the same way as shown here, and thus they will not be described. In case of
RRT∗, the cost is calculated the same way, but instead of the heuristic distance cost,
the neighbor distance is used. At every rewiring step this cost is re-calculated for the
two nodes, that the rewiring mechanism currently considers.
For the sampling-based RRT ∗ the same exact sampling settings will be used as
for the proposed extended method which will be explained in the next section. As
the map is 48m x 48m with 0.18cm cell size, it has a total of 71289 cells. For this
size, 20000 samples are used. Using a bit more than 1/4th of the cells to sample
was a good balance between performance and planning time during tests. Using
much more samples did not have an equally better outcome, while it impacted the
performance significantly. The step size of the sampling is set to the length of the
robot’s footprint, which is about 0.7cm. This value is not so important in the case
of the classical method but will play an important role in the extended method and
will be explained in the next section.

To implement an extended version of RRT∗ first a copy of the Ai-Winter’s RRT∗ im-

If the requested position is inside the grid map, the height value is applied to the point

at least the size of the virtual triangle footprint for safety. Nonetheless, this case
will have to be considered in future work, as the map might not always be perfectly
uniform due to holes in the perception. The function .atPosition() returns the cell
height the given position falls into. If the position is at an intersection of cells, then
the nearest cell is taken. This is a limitation of the approach, as the quality of the
roll and pitch calculations are limited by the resolution of the map. The finer the
map resolution the more accurate the height taken at an arbitrary position will be.
If a finer resolution than the dimensions of the wheel of the robot is used, then an
averaged height of the covered area can be taken.
After this calculation, the triangle now lays on the 2.5D surface, representing the
robot’s possible orientation on the terrain.
To get the pitch and roll values of the triangle the normal vector of its surface is
taken. To do these calculations the Eigen [64] library is used by the example of other
papers, as it is a well-established and efficient mathematical library.
The points are first converted into Eigen’s Vector3D representation:
1 Eigen::Vector3d p3_(urp1.x, urp1.y, p1.z);
2 Eigen::Vector3d p2_(urp3.x, urp3.y , p3.z);
3 Eigen::Vector3d p1_(urp4.x, urp4.y, p4.z);
Here the name ”urp“ refers to the non-rotated state of the points. The rotation of the
points to project the triangle was only necessary for taking the height of each point
at the projected angle, however, to get the edges the original triangle can be used
to simplify calculations. This approach works, because the pitch and roll values are
only needed in the robot’s own reference frame. Now, using two edges of the triangle,
two vectors are formed and the cross-product is taken, and normalized to have the
magnitude 1:
1 Eigen::Vector3d Edge1 = p2_ - p1_;
2 Eigen::Vector3d Edge2 = p3_ - p1_;
3 Eigen::Vector3d cross_ = Edge1.cross(Edge2);
4 cross_.normalize();
To calculate the pitch and roll of the normal vector, the following function explained
in Section 2.2.4 Equation 3.3 can be used:
1 pitch = std::atan2(cross_.x(), cross_.z());
2 roll = -std::atan2(cross_.y(), cross_.z());

Similarly to the paper shown in section 2.2.2, the part of the cost that considers roll
and pitch at a given position n is calculated as shown in equation 3.4. To save the
calculated values a custom struct has been created containing both roll and pitch. In

1 return ((abs(orientation.roll)/N)*W_roll + (abs(orientation.pitch)/N)*
W_pitch)*W_slope;
The W (weight) values have been adjusted empirically during testing for best per-
formance, however, due to their sensitivity there weren’t many options to keep the
distance and orientation costs in equilibrium. Slightly higher weights on the roll and
pitch resulted in much longer paths, while slightly lower weights resulted in danger-
ous paths being planned. Moreover, the pitch has been given less weight than roll, as
going straight up a slope is less dangerous than having a high amount of roll on e.g.
the side of a hill.
During the sampling phase, additionally to the collision check an initial feasibility
check is performed using the same footprint-based method, which returns an infinitely
high cost if roll or pitch is above a threshold value of 27 degrees at the given yaw.
This means, that the maximum, lethal value is the same for this algorithm as for
the classical 2D planning methods on the costmap. There is however an important
difference between this threshold and the lethal cost of the classical method using a
2D costmap. This technique does not make path planning impossible over 27-degree
slopes, but simply forces the extended RRT ∗ to continue sampling, as it would in
case of a collision. Given enough samples, the algorithm might still find a path, that
is slightly skewed to the slope, making the roll and pitch values less than 27 degrees,
and thus producing a plan on the slope successfully, and within safe bounds.
75
--- PAGE 85 ---
Now that the contribution to the algorithm is defined, the integration into the base
cost function will be explained. It has been decided, that the algorithm’s original flow
of checking for collision will be kept regardless of the new cost function, but with a
much higher lethal threshold. This has the advantage, that parts of the map, which
wouldn’t be traversable at any angle because of their steepness will be skipped and
the cost function will not be calculated, increasing performance. This means that the
designed approach is actually multi-layered, using both a 2D costmap to check for
initial feasibility, and using the 2.5D grid map natively to calculate the costs. The
new threshold is thus set to 32 degrees of slope, which has been defined empirically
during testing and can be adjusted at any time. This slope could not be traversed by
the robot at any approach angle.
The cost calculation used by the original function is the standard cost function of
RRT∗, finding the nearest neighbor by distance. Additionally to distance, here the
proposed slope cost is used. The cost to traverse from node qn to qp is then defined
as follows:
cost(qn, qp) = dist(qn, qp) + C(qp) (4.1)
where C(qp) is calculated as defined in Equation 3.4 and represents the orientation-

follows:
1 double new_dist = std::hypot(parent.x()-new_node.x(), parent.y()-
new_node.y());
2 double cost = parent.g()+new_dist+slopeCost(parent, new_node);
The slopeCost function takes both the parent and the new node in order to calculate
the angle between them:
1 double angle = atan2((parent.y() - new_node.y()),(parent.x()-
new_node.x()));
This angle is then directly used for the rotation of the footprint-triangle projected
onto the height map.
With RRT∗ at every iteration of tree expansion, the neighbors of a new node are
checked inside a so-called ”optimization circle “ if a connection with less overall cost
can be found. Thus the algorithm converges to the least overall cost as shown in
Section 2.2.2, Equation 2.13 and 2.14. Based on Equations 2.13 and 2.14 in theory
with an infinite number of nodes this solution would converge to a set of waypoints
with the best balance between the least amount of pitch and roll variation and total
distance.

4.2.1 Generating terrain
Terrain generation was done using Blender as described in Section 2.4.2, using a plane
object and geometry nodes. The method is simple but requires knowledge of the func-
tions of Blender. Due to previous experience with Blender, it was a convenient way
to generate the terrain, as it allows to export the object in any popular 3D format.
Thus, it is possible to export it in any other representation if future works require it.
The process involves creating a 50m x 50m plane and subdividing it into cells. The
number of subdivisions determines the quality of the generated object. It also plays
a role during the extraction of the pointcloud, as a higher number of subdivisions
results in more points at the end. This means, that the resolution of the grid should
be finer than that of the resulting grid map to avoid holes. First, the subdivision
resolution was determined empirically, by looking at the quality of the generated ter-
rain. After some experimentation it became clear, that the number of subdivisions
needed exceeds the resolution of the grid map by orders of magnitude, thus the exact
number isn’t relevant, as lots of data points fall into each cell, even though only one
per cell would be required to generate a map without holes.

4.2.3 Implementing the tests script and evaluation
Due to the large number of tests and measurements to be recorded a Python script
has been written to automate the process. This script uses along with other com-
panion data-gathering scripts the information directly from CoppeliaSim and ROS to
record evaluation data.
As described in the previous section, additional code was added to the Husky’s script
in CoppeliaSim to publish torque and energy data. These values are published every
time the simulation steps forward and are saved in an array. When the simulation
is started the array is always reset to 0 elements. In CoppeliaSim if the simulation
is completely stopped, and not just paused, it resets every value in the environment,
and also the position of the robot back to the start. This can be used to the advantage
of testing, as CoppeliaSim’s ROS interface can be controlled using action commands
from a ROS node.
Each terrain had a total number of 5 goal positions for each start position to
ensure good coverage of the terrain. Each terrain was used twice, where the start
positions were placed in opposite corners. This changes the path planning problem
completely from the perspective of the algorithm, effectively leading to 5 terrains with
10 planning problems each. As no good way was found to determine the goal and
start positions on an arbitrary, random terrain, they were defined manually, making
sure that they were actually reachable. In future work ways of finding good start and
goal positions automatically will be researched to be able to automate this process as
well. An example of an array of goal positions used by the test script looks as follows:

1 node_process = Popen(shlex.split(’roslaunch planner planning.launch

This command starts a new terminal process and executes the given command, which

cess is started, another ROS node is executed which is a listener written in C++,
that calculates the robot’s roll, pitch, and yaw continuously and publishes them as a
float array to a ROS topic. The listener uses the ground truth odometry information
published by CoppeliaSim. The script then publishes the next coordinates from the
array of goal positions defined above and records both the current simulation time
and system time. This is necessary because the simulation time in CoppeliaSim is set
to 5x faster to save time, but the time it took to return a plan still has to be recorded
accurately by using the real system time. After a path is generated, a callback func-
tion in the script records the time.
After this, no other commands are published, and only statistics are recorded until
the robot reaches the goal. The goal tolerance has been set to 0.5m, which is slightly
higher than the controller’s goal tolerance, to ensure that each test can be finished
cleanly without the robot struggling to get closer to the goal. There is a timeout of
6 Minutes of simulation time, after which the goal is considered unreached and the
iteration continues with the next goal. This scenario can happen if the generated
path leads to the robot getting stuck. At the end of each iteration, the continuously
recorded arrays, roll pitch, yaw, and torque are saved as a Numpy [69] array file
for later analysis, and initial plots are saved using the matplotlib [70] library. All
other statistics, like path length, planning time, and peak values are stored in a .csv

(Comma separated values) file using the Pandas [71] framework. In sum, for each

lems for 5 terrains and 7 files for each path, a total of 1750 data files are produced

To evaluate these results another automation script has been used, as it wouldn’t
be feasible to go through them manually in a program like Excel. The evaluation

deviation values for roll, pitch, yaw, and torque for each path and also averaged for
each terrain and concatenates them to the other values (the peak values, path length,

path also has the advantage, that other evaluation metrics can be developed and used

It is expected, that the execution of the path produced by some of the algorithms
might fail. It is unlikely, that all algorithms produce an undrivable plan for the same
goals and same terrains. This means, that a fair comparison would not be possible
across all algorithms, because some of them would have more data on different ter-

as these methods use random sampling, and will produce slightly different solutions
every time. Here again, only the terrains and paths are averaged, where the test
succeeded across all terrains and paths for each try. This is not necessary for the rest,
as all other algorithms tested are deterministic, and will plan the exact same path

Chapter 6
Conclusions and Future Work
In this thesis methods for path planning on uneven terrain have been discussed and
evaluated. Moreover, an extension to use native 2.5D information was proposed. This
thesis presents a comprehensive research on path planning methods for complex envi-
ronments. In this work, we focused on one aspect of off-road path planning, namely
the techniques required to plan paths on uneven terrain. Most of the research dis-
cussed, especially the pipelines in Sections 2.2.3, 2.2.4, 2.2.1 present their works as
complete systems, from global planning to mission execution. This aspect is partic-
ularly interesting for us, as we intend to integrate advance path planning into our
robot navigation system. Investigating such complete pipelines is beyond the scope
of this thesis. Thus, the work has been restricted to path planning on 2.5D surfaces
in a global fashion. The goal of this investigation was to gain a better understanding
of the state-of-the-art in this domain and common techniques used in the field and
to evaluate promising solutions in close-to-life, simulated environments.
The contribution of this thesis included an overview of commonly used methods and
algorithms for planning on uneven surfaces, the setup of standard, search-based plan-
ning algorithms for 2D costmap-based planning, implementation of an extended plan-
ning solution using native 2.5D information, and an evaluation pipeline, using a real-
istic simulation method. The evaluation showed somewhat mixed results across the
board of algorithms. However, it can be concluded, that the proposed contribution to
RRT∗ shows promising results, and already achieves better performance in the tested
scenarios than using the classical baseline 2D planning methods.
6.1 Conclusion
The evaluations provided us with some valuable information regarding planning on
uneven terrain. It has been shown, that using 2.5D information is beneficial for plan-
ning in off-road scenarios by evaluating different metrics recorded during testing. It
has also been concluded, that the proposed approach is less computationally effi-
cient than methods using standard search-based algorithms on a 2D costmap. Thus,
99
--- PAGE 109 ---
there is still work to be done for the controbitued 2.5D-based planner to be usable
in real-time with a real robot. It has also been shown, that using a compressed 2D
representation of the environment along with a classical search algorithm is a viable
option if the map processing is done properly, respecting the worst-case boundaries of
the robot. The main benefit of this way of planning is the easy setup, expandability
and good performance on underpowered hardware. To convert the 2.5D grid map
to the 2D costmap representation various filtering techniques can be used. In this
thesis, one of the most basic and widely used methods has been used, by converting
the terrain data into a slope map. The steepness of a slope is a good representation
of risk for a rigid-body mobile robot in most cases. In reality, the slope map could be
extended by additional traversability costs to also account for different surface types
and roughness levels. Meanwhile, the proposed method using 2.5D data has great
potential of combining the cost calculation on a 2.5D surface with other map layers.
As the proposed method is only a proof-of-concept solution it still requires more work
to achieve a better quality outcome. As shown in Figure 5.12 the algorithm has to
be extended with some bias for sampling more densely in the close proximity of the
robot to ensure better quality paths on short range, while keeping a good enough,
but coarser path at higher distances.
6.2 Future work
T urning on slopes
As discussed in the previous sections, the path execution failed in most of the cases
for one reason: turning on slopes. The authors of RAMP [13] discussed in Section
2.2.3 present this problem as one of the main issues using rigid body differential-drive
robots. Turning on a slope artificially decreases traction on all wheels in such robots,
as they use the decreased friction to turn, which completely changes their dynamics
momentarily. This is however also a problem with Ackermann-drive robots as a sharp
turn on a slope shifts the center of weight to one side, which decreases the tipping an-
gle significantly, regardless of robot type. The authors of RAMP [13] solve this issue
by restricting yaw deviations above a certain slope angle. In future work, something
similar will be investigated further. Using a slope map together with the proposed
extended RRT∗ method, initial path planning could still be done using the 2.5D data
as presented, and the slope cost on the 2D costmap could be incorporated into the
optimization process of an MPC-based controller, penalizing yaw deviations when the
robot is driving on a steep slope. The lookahead distance of such model predictive
controllers can be adjusted to an arbitrary value. Choosing a long enough lookahead
distance would give a chance to the controller to adjust its approach angle before a
slope in order to avoid yaw deviation while on the slope. With such a controller,
multiple time horizons also explained in the paper RAMP [13] could be introduced
for places on the map that don’t have enough information.

However, yaw deviations on slopes could also be restricted by the proposed plan-
ning method as well, as it has all the data necessary to do so. It is however unclear,
how such an additional check would impact planning performance and must be in-
vestigated further.
Extension with configuration space
The Artus presented in the introduction is a more capable off-road robot than the
Husky. While the Husky has a rigid body and non-turntable wheels the Artus can
turn each wheel individually, and also raise and lower them by some amount. With
such capabilities much more is possible regarding the traversal of challenging terrains.
Simply said, the robot can be configured in different ways to change its capabilities
drastically. The possible configurations of such a robot are usually mapped to a
so-called configuration space or C-space. A configuration space is a map of all the
states the robot can achieve safely within its bounds. The configuration space can
not only represent obstacle collisions but also could be used to search for generally
valid configurations for the robot in the presence of some constraints. The idea is,
to combine the proposed sampling method with a search in the configuration space.
The algorithm would still use the footprint of the robot on the 2.5D map, but search
the C-space of the robot at any given point to find the best possible settings to
traverse the given section or to find configurations that make it possible to traverse
an otherwise untraversable area. For this to work properly, the edges between two
sampling points must also be incorporated into the planning process, such that the
robot transitions from one configuration into the other just in time. Transitioning too
quickly or too late might lead to dangerous situations. An example of this would be a
hill that has to be traversed on the side, which would be out-of-bounds for the default
configuration, but the Artus could slightly lower one set of wheels to compensate for
the angle, and bring its center of mass back to a safe state. If this transition happens
too late or too early, then the robot might tip over.
Such a planning pipeline would allow for more options during planning in complex,
off-road conditions, and would potentially allow for paths, that would otherwise not
be deemed traversable. The performance of such an approach is however questionable
as multiple objectives would have be integrated in one search problem. Nonetheless,
such a method will be tested in future work for viability.
6.2.1 Segmentation
It was mentioned previously, that both the 2D costmap and the 2.5D-based methods
could adjust their bounds and cost values dynamically at runtime. This property
could be used to introduce additional information during planning. Using a camera
on the robot, the terrain directly in front could be segmented into different categories.
There are many machine-learning models currently under development around this
topic. One of the most notable ones was developed by Valada et al. from the Au-
tonomous Intelligent Systems at TU Freiburg, called DeepScene [73]. Using such a
101
--- PAGE 111 ---
neural network the terrain in front of the robot could be segmented into different
surface types. The segmentation could then be used to adjust the bounds for plan-
ning. If for example, the segmented terrain changes from a gravel trail to grass, the
planner adjusts its bounds to allow for less roll and pitch deviation, as slippage is
more probable on grass. The controller could also be notified of this, slowing down
the robot on dangerous surfaces. The main challenge in this approach is to accurately
match the segmented surface with the 2.5D surface mapped using the LIDAR. This
method could also be used to create an additional layer on the grid map which could
be considered as an additional traversability cost during planning.
6.2.2 Controller-only solution
In the project described in the introduction, the Artus will mostly follow a very
roughly drawn path, defined a priori on a map, to make sure, that it follows the
soldier’s trails. The proposed solution can thus be thought of as a local planner
rather than a global one. However, if a high-powered computing solution is used
on the Artus, the calculations could potentially be done in the optimization step
of an optimization-based controller like MPC. In this case, the proposed slope cost
presented in this thesis would have to be reformulated to represent an additional
equation in the optimization problem, or maybe even divided into multiple smaller
problems with separate optimization objectives. This solution will only work effi-
ciently if the time it takes to do the additional optimization step doesn’t overpower
the time and path length constraints of the controller. To see if such a solution is
a viable option some tests will be conducted using a simpler optimization objective,
and only introduce the full 2.5D map if the performance is good enough.
6.2.3 Direction-vector-based slope-map
The ideas presented so far all propose some additional cost-based calculation as an
extension. However, it would also be possible to do some of the work during the
mapping process and prepare the map in a way, such that it provides more information
for the path planner. As the grid map library supports multiple layers, this could be
exploited to create 2 separate layers: one layer could be a simple slope map and the
second layer could use a custom data structure to associate a direction vector to each
slope value. A similar idea is used by the authors in MeshNavigation [9], however,
they use their pre-calculated vector field to guide the robot to the goal, and not for
slope direction. In this case, the planners would plan on a 2D costmap, and only check
the direction vectors of the slope if it is a viable option for traversal. For example, A ∗
could be extended to do an initial check if the visited cell is within general bounds
and then use the direction vector to calculate the actual cell cost. If the direction
vector of the slope is the same as the direction of planning, then a lower cost could
be introduced, as going up or down a slope facing the direction of the slope is much
easier for any robot. This cost could also be used to calculate the effort needed and
risk associated with a turn on a slope, making a more educated decision than simply
102
--- PAGE 112 ---
using a worst-case cost as presented in this thesis. The most important advantage
of this method would be efficiency, as the calculations needed to make an additional
map layer and check the vector direction would be much less expensive than doing
full 2.5D terrain-based checks. This method would however still require some degree
of compression, as rough surfaces would still loose detail during this process. For this
reason, this method will only be considered as a plan-B if the native 2.5D planning
method isn’t efficient enough for real world use.
